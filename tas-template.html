Elevation: LIST Tasmania 25 m DEM (Land Tasmania, CC BY 3.0 AU; TIN of 1:25k 10 m contours, spot heights and water bodies; 90% within 5 m), exactly area-averaged to the chosen stud size. Blocks holding a LIST spot height take the surveyed value. Lakes: LIST Hydrographic Areas at full-supply level; a block is water when ≥50% covered. Sea = cells outside the DEM (heights are AHD, ≈ mean sea level). Grid is MGA zone 55. Labels from LIST Named Features and spot heights. Urban areas: LIST Landuse Live (ALUM 5.3–5.9 intensive uses excl. rural residential and recreation), shown as flat tiles where a block is ≥50% urban. Other surface colours are a slope/elevation classification, not mapped vegetation.

Geology: Mineral Resources Tasmania digital geology, 1:25 000 (1:250 000 where unmapped), via theLIST (CC BY 3.0 AU). The walls and build-step slices show rock; Geology map also replaces the vegetation plate with the mapped unit. Below ground is an indicative 2.5D model: bases of the near-flat Jurassic dolerite and Permian–Triassic (Parmeener) layers are fitted to the DEM elevation of mapped contacts (a plane of ≤3° dip plus a local correction that fades over ~600 m), then forced to agree with the surface map in every cell; older folded basement is extended vertically from the nearest mapped outcrop; glacial and other Quaternary cover is a 10 m veneer. Faults are not yet modelled. Use the build-step slider to slice through it.

Buildings: Overture Maps Foundation, buildings theme release 2026-09-23.0 (footprints mostly OpenStreetMap, © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL). Height = building height where given (mostly Microsoft ML estimates), raised to 3.3 m × floors where floors are tagged; else a default by type (houses 5 m, sheds 3 m, other 8 m). A 25 m cell is built when ≥4 of 16 sub-samples fall in a footprint and takes the tallest building there; coarser blocks take the mean of their built cells when ≥25% built. Buildings sit on top of the terrain; none on flooded cells. Indicative only.

Ice age: glacier outlines from the GLACIMONTIS compilation of reconstructed palaeoglaciers (Zenodo, doi:10.5281/zenodo.15600659, CC BY 4.0), which for Tasmania maps the maximum ice extent of the last glaciation from Colhoun & Barrows (2001), Barrows et al. (2002) and Mackintosh et al. (2006). Ice thickness is not mapped: it is modelled as perfectly plastic ice (basal shear stress 100 kPa, ρ = 900 kg m−3) on today’s ground surface, built inwards from the mapped margin, so it is indicative only (no isostasy, lakes not removed from the bed).

Fire history: theLIST Fire History – All (Tasmanian Government fire agencies, via theLIST, CC BY 3.0 AU): mapped perimeters of bushfires and planned burns by fire season (July–June). A round tile marks every block whose most recent recorded fire is within 60 years (bushfires) or 20 years (planned burns), coloured by years before the current season; it sits on the canopy where there is one. A 25 m cell counts as burnt when ≥50% of it lies inside a perimeter; coarser blocks follow the majority of their 25 m cells. Perimeters include unburnt patches, and records before the 1990s are incomplete.

Water drop: the drop follows the steepest descent over the block heights shown (D8 routing on a depression-filled surface: Priority-Flood with ε, Barnes et al. 2014), so paths and catchments are only as good as the chosen stud size. Wet sea cells and the model edge are outlets; lakes are crossed at their outlet. The red outline encloses every block that drains to the same outlet. Shown on the 1 km All of Tasmania scene only.

Sea-level rise is a static, uniform (“bathtub”) rise with hydraulic connectivity to the sea, computed on the metre heights before brick quantisation. It ignores ocean-dynamic and gravitational fingerprints, vertical land motion/GIA, tides and surge. The DEM interpolates between the 1 m coastline and the first 10 m contour, so rises of a few metres are not reliably resolved; use LiDAR for planning. Sea floor: AusBathyTopo (Australia) 250 m 2026 (Geoscience Australia, CC BY 4.0), heights relative to mean sea level, bilinear to 25 m, shown to a floor of −150 m. Lowering the sea exposes sea floor connected to open water at the model edge; enclosed basins are shown dry. Today’s sea floor is used for past sea levels (no correction for sediment, isostasy or tectonics).
